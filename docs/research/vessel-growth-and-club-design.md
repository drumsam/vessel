# How the list grows — referral, club design and word of mouth

7 October 2026. Researched from the platforms that actually did it, applied to VESSEL.

---

## 1 · What worked elsewhere, with the numbers

**Monzo — the golden ticket.** After two weeks of active use, a customer got **one**
invitation that let someone skip the waitlist. Not money — queue-jumping power.
**About 40% of all 2017 signups came through golden tickets**, at almost no
acquisition cost. Monzo went from 70,000 to 650,000 users that year with minimal
paid marketing. The alpha was deliberately capped at 3,000.

The insight: *the reward was the thing the user already wanted — access — and
giving it away made the giver look generous rather than like an affiliate.*

**Robinhood — the queue as a product.** Sign-ups saw their **exact position in
line**, and referring moved them up it. Nearly **one million people** joined the
waitlist in the year before launch. The referral changed the one number the user
was already checking.

**Clubhouse — invites as status, and the lesson in the bust.** Two invites per
member made membership feel like an achievement, and built a waitlist in the
millions. It also showed the failure mode: when the room opened wide, the quality
fell and the habit died. Scarcity has to protect something real.

**Whatnot — two-sided credit, carefully bounded.** Referrer and referred both get
credit; the referrer is only paid **after the new buyer's first order is
delivered**. Credits expire in 90 days, apply to subtotal only, and are capped at
$10,000 lifetime. Variants include randomised ($10–$200) and tiered rewards.

**Soho House — the cap as the product.** By late 2023 the waitlist hit **98,000**
and membership was growing 21% a quarter — so in December 2023 they **stopped
admitting new members in London, New York and LA** because members said the
houses felt too busy. They chose member experience over growth.

**Dropbox** — the canonical two-sided referral: both sides got the same thing
(storage), which made the ask frictionless and the reward obviously fair.

## 2 · What transfers to wine, and what doesn't

**Transfers:**

- **Access as currency.** Our scarce asset is first look at good cellars, not
  entry to a website. That is exactly Monzo's golden ticket.
- **Pay on completion, not on signup.** Whatnot's rule. Paying for a sign-up buys
  tourists; paying for a completed purchase buys buyers.
- **Visible position.** A waitlist number, and referring moves you up it.
- **A real cap.** Soho House's lesson, in reverse: cap *before* the room degrades,
  not after.

**Doesn't transfer:**

- **Cash credits as the headline.** A £25 credit cheapens a HK$69,500 purchase and
  trains people to wait for discounts. Our price conviction is the brand; never
  undercut it with a coupon.
- **Public referral codes.** Discoverable codes mean the "20% below market" claim
  becomes "20% below market, plus whatever code I can find". Invitations must be
  private and personal.
- **Volume for volume's sake.** 10,000 members would kill the thing. The drop has
  to feel findable, not picked over.

## 3 · The explicit offer — actual copy

### Buyer invites buyer

> **You have one invitation.**
> Someone you'd happily sit next to at dinner. They skip the waitlist.
> When they buy for the first time, you both get **first look on the next three
> drops** — one hour before anyone else.

No cash. The reward is the only thing members actually want, and it costs nothing
except an hour of exclusivity we control. (Optional sweetener if conversion is
slow: **1% of their first purchase** as credit against the buyer's next one —
affordable against 8% and still not a coupon.)

### Seller refers seller — pay properly here

Supply is the constraint, so this is where real money belongs.

> **Introduce a cellar.**
> If they sell through VESSEL, we pay you **1% of their proceeds**, and we cover
> **their first transfer and storage costs** so the introduction costs them
> nothing.

On a £200,000 cellar that's £2,000 out of roughly £16,000 of margin, for supply
you'd otherwise spend months finding. It is the best-value marketing in the
business.

### The founding cohort

> **First 100 members.**
> Permanent first look, for as long as you're a member. We'll never offer it
> again.

Costs nothing, creates the early advocates, and gives the first hundred a reason
to recruit the second hundred.

### The waitlist

> **You're number 214.**
> Each introduction moves you up 20 places. Members are admitted in order, as
> capacity allows.

Robinhood's mechanic exactly: the referral moves the number they're already
watching.

## 4 · Club design — cap it, and say why

**Cap by invitation flow, not by a hard ceiling.** A fixed "500 members" invites
argument. "Every member may introduce one person a quarter" governs growth
naturally, keeps it earned, and makes the invitation valuable.

**How many buyers does a drop actually need?**

- 30 lines a week, target 70% sold within 48 hours ≈ **21 sales**.
- If 5% of active members buy on any given drop, that needs **~420 active buyers**.
- At 10%, **~210**.

So the first-year target is **200–400 active buyers**, and the right ceiling is
around **500 members**. Past that, either the drops get bigger or the room gets
worse. State the cap publicly: *"We admit members only as fast as we find
cellars."* That sentence does two jobs — it explains the scarcity and it tells
every member that introducing a cellar is what opens the door for their friends.

**Earned status, never purchased:**

| Tier | How you get there | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Member** | invited, verified | the Thursday drop |
| **Private** | three purchases, or one cellar introduced | drop one hour early, one invitation a quarter |
| **First** | top buyers and sellers each year | first look on every new cellar before the drop |

No fee. Status by activity, which is what makes the room good — and it's the one
place I'd differ from Crurated's €300.

## 5 · How word actually spreads in wine

**The dinner moment.** Someone opens a bottle with three friends and says what
they paid. That is the single highest-conversion moment in this business, and it
happens with no product involvement. Give it something to hold: a card on the
purchase page — what this wine is, why this vintage, how to serve it, what it
costs at a merchant — that's worth showing at the table. "Look like a pro" is a
growth mechanic, not a vanity feature.

**Storage-facility clusters.** Collectors who store at the same place know each
other — LCB and Octavian in the UK, the big HK facilities. One happy seller in a
building is a warm introduction to five more. Ask every seller which facility
they use, and track it.

**The seller testimonial with numbers.** Not "great service". **"42 lines,
£46,000, paid in eleven days, I did nothing but tick boxes."** That is the
sentence that brings the next cellar, and it costs you nothing but permission to
use it.

**The forwardable Thursday link.** Every drop link is personal and trackable, and
the page says: *"Forward this to one person. If they join and buy, you both get
first look."* The share is a feature of the thing they're already looking at.

**Published results, selectively.** After each drop, members see what sold and at
what price. It proves the pricing honest, creates fear of missing the next one,
and makes the whole system legible — the Bring a Trailer effect, where the
public record is the marketing.

## 6 · Guardrails

- **Never a public discount code.** It destroys price conviction, which is the
  asset.
- **Never pay for a sign-up.** Pay on a completed first purchase, like Whatnot.
- **Never let the room outgrow the supply.** Soho House had to close three
  cities; design the cap in from the start instead.
- **Invitations are personal and private.** No codes in forums.
- **One invitation at a time.** Unused for a quarter, it expires — which makes
  people use it.

---

## 7 · What to measure

- **Invitations sent, and the share accepted.**
- **Referred member → first purchase**, and how many days it took.
- **Cellars introduced by members** — the one that matters most, because supply is
  the constraint.
- **Share of each drop sold in 48 hours** (the room's health).
- **Active buyer count per drop**, against the 200–400 target.

One composite worth watching monthly: **members per cellar**. If it climbs,
you're accumulating an audience faster than supply and the drops will thin out.
If it falls, you need more buyers before the next cellar lands.

---

## 8 · Ninety days

- **Weeks 1–2.** Founding cohort: hand-pick 20 buyers, tell them they're the
  first hundred, give each one invitation.
- **Weeks 3–4.** First drop. Publish results to members afterwards.
- **Weeks 5–6.** Seller referral live, with the 1% and covered transfer costs.
  Tell every seller, in the approval screen, that it exists.
- **Weeks 7–8.** Waitlist with position, and the move-up-20-places mechanic.
- **Weeks 9–12.** Status tiers switched on, earned retrospectively from actual
  activity so nobody starts at zero.
