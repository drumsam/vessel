# How to move cellars: mechanisms, platforms, and a plan

Research report, 6 October 2026. Written for the UK/HK fine wine brokerage.

---

## Part 1 · What the best new entrants actually did

Five companies rebuilt how illiquid, multi-line, trust-dependent inventory gets
sold. None of them won on inventory. They won on **mechanism**.

### Whatnot — the cadence is the product

Live sales: **$3bn (2024) → $8bn (2025) → more than $8bn in the first half of
2026 alone**. 650,000 new accounts a week by August 2026. One in eight sellers
earns a full-time living on it. Series G in August 2026: $545m at a $20bn
valuation.

What they understood: collectors don't want a catalogue, they want an **event**.
A scheduled, hosted, time-boxed show where things sell in front of you. The
scarcity is not in the goods, it's in the moment. Fifty lots in an hour, with a
human talking, beats a thousand lots sitting on a website forever.

### Bring a Trailer — truth is a pricing strategy

Hagerty measured it: cars sold on BaT fetched **36% above guide value
(2016–19), 55% in 2020, 72% in 2021**. Cars flipped from other venues onto BaT
within a year made **24% more**. Same car, same market, different venue.

The premium comes from obsessive honesty — every flaw photographed and
described, a 450,000-strong community interrogating each listing in public, and
comments that stay attached to the car forever. **The disclosure *is* what
raises the price**, because it removes the buyer's discount for uncertainty.

### StockX — the number is a market, not an opinion

Live bids and asks on every item. A seller doesn't ask "what's it worth?", they
look at the highest bid and decide. The spread is visible. No negotiation, no
haggling theatre, no feeling of being worked over. Transparency replaced trust:
you don't need to trust a dealer if you can see the order book.

### Fanatics Collect — liquidity on demand, three speeds

One vault, three exits: a **weekly auction** (everything closes Sunday
evenings), a **monthly premier sale** for items above $10,000, and a **Buy Now
marketplace** where "the price you see is the price you pay" — no buyer fees,
and a **6% seller fee** on anything listed below 120% of market. Items sit in the
vault and the owner picks a speed: instant, weekly, or monthly.

That is the key structural idea. **Custody plus multiple exit speeds equals
liquidity.** The vault isn't storage, it's the thing that makes one-tap selling
possible.

### Crurated — membership as the filter

**€300 a year**, applications reviewed individually, allocations direct from
producers rather than auctions, and professional storage treated as part of the
promise rather than an add-on. Every bottle gets a verifiable identity and every
movement in custody is documented.

They sell *access and certainty*, not wine. The membership fee does something
subtle: it pre-qualifies the buyer, so the seller side never deals with tourists.

### And two adjacent mechanics worth stealing

**Faire and Ankorstore** (B2B wholesale) took the risk out of the buy side with
net-60 terms and free returns on first orders. The retailer's fear isn't price,
it's being stuck with stock.

**B-Stock** sells pallets by **manifest** — you bid on a structured list of
contents, not on a photo. That's the closest existing analogue to a cellar: a
multi-line lot, sold as a lot, where the manifest is the product description.

## Part 2 · The pattern underneath

Seven mechanisms recur across every winner:

1. **A visible reference price.** Not a dealer's opinion. A number with a source.
2. **Disclosure as a premium, not a liability.** Say what's wrong and get paid
   more, not less.
3. **One-tap liquidity.** The seller chooses certainty (instant, lower) or
   patience (auction, higher). Both are offered; the choice is theirs.
4. **Custody.** Whoever holds the stock controls the speed of the transaction.
5. **Membership that filters.** A closed room with vetted members on both sides.
6. **Event cadence.** Weekly or monthly, same day, every time. Habit beats reach.
7. **A link, not an attachment.** The offer is a live page that updates, tracks,
   and can be shared — never a PDF or a spreadsheet in an inbox.

Email isn't dead as plumbing; it's dead as the *product*. Nobody evaluates
£50,000 of wine inside an email thread. The email is just the doorbell.

## Part 3 · What this looks like in wine today

The current experience of selling a cellar, from the seller's side:

1. Email a spreadsheet to three merchants.
2. Wait a week or three.
3. Get back a single lump-sum number, or a spreadsheet with a column of prices
   and no evidence whatsoever.
4. Have no idea whether it's fair. Accept out of fatigue, or go to auction and
   wait four months to find out.
5. Discover on collection that some lines are "re-assessed".

Every incumbent does roughly this. Cru Wine, Cult Wines, Vinfolio, K&L, Sokolin
all offer "expert valuation, global buyer access, secure logistics" — all three
of which are assertions, not evidence. **The entire category competes on
reassurance.** Nobody shows their working.

That is the opening, and it's a wide one, because showing the working is exactly
what the pricing engine already does.

## Part 4 · Through the lens of the founders who built those businesses

What a Whatnot or BaT founder would say, looking at this business:

**"Your product isn't the price, it's the certainty."** Sellers don't lie awake
wondering whether they got 73% or 76% of market. They lie awake wondering
whether they're being had. Sell certainty and the margin stops being contested.

**"Productise the offer, don't negotiate it."** The moment a price becomes a
conversation, you've added a week and lost the advantage. One link, both numbers,
every line, accept or decline per line.

**"Make the bargain legible."** Spell it out in the seller's own arithmetic: the
market is £62,386, buyers in this trade move parcels 20% below market, our fee is
8%, you receive £45,916, which is 73.6%. Printed openly, that reads as a
professional spread. Hidden, the same number reads as a fleece.

**"Pick a cadence and never miss it."** A Thursday list that goes out every
Thursday builds a buying habit. Habit is what turns one deal into a business.

**"The hard side is the buyers, so build the seller experience."** Doug has the
buyers. The constraint is cellars — so the product to perfect is the *seller's*
experience, and the thing that spreads is sellers telling other sellers.

**"Charge for speed, not for access."** Two prices on every cellar: paid now,
lower; paid on sale, higher. Let the seller choose. That single choice removes
most negotiation.

## Part 5 · The specific position here

**Assets:** an evidence-based pricing engine that already traces every figure to
a named merchant offer; buyers who transact at 20% below market; 8% on sales;
reach across UK and Hong Kong bond; one trusted seller cellar already in hand
(561 lines, 3,544 bottles) and "many clients who need to move wine on".

**Constraints, honestly:** no custody of your own; thin market evidence in
Burgundy (4% of lines price on two merchants, against 36% in Bordeaux); a
one-person operation where every hour of manual matching is the bottleneck; no
buyer-side demand signal yet.

**The asymmetry that matters:** every competitor says "trust us". You can say
"here is the offer, here is every merchant price behind it, here are the lines we
could not price and why". Nobody in fine wine does this. It is a defensible
difference and it compounds, because each cellar priced makes the evidence base
better.

## Part 6 · What excites people — the list

Ranked by how much pull each one has, for this market:

1. **"Here's what it's actually worth, with proof."** The single biggest
   unlock. A seller who sees four merchant prices per line stops haggling.
2. **Money this week.** An instant-offer option on part of the cellar — the
   liquid lines — at a slightly wider spread. Speed sells.
3. **A number that moves.** "Your cellar: £412,000 today, up 4% this quarter."
   Collectors check portfolios like share prices.
4. **A room you were let into.** Membership, vetting, an application. Scarcity of
   access, not of product.
5. **Watching the market clear.** A Thursday live list where lines go in front
   of other members. Entertainment and price discovery at once.
6. **Being told what to drink and what to sell.** "These twelve are at peak and
   fully valued; drink these, sell those." Advice that costs the adviser money is
   the most persuasive kind.
7. **No downside.** Free valuation, no obligation, no collection charge if they
   decline, and the valuation document is theirs to keep.
8. **Sealed, not haggled.** One link, per-line accept or decline, no phone calls.
9. **Provenance that travels with the bottle.** Storage history, condition, the
   whole chain — which raises the resale price later, as BaT's disclosure
   premium proves.
10. **Being first.** Buyers get a 24-hour window on new cellars before the list
    goes wide. Allocation feels like status.

## Part 7 · The build — strategies and tools

### Strategy 1 · The Offer Room (replaces the email and the spreadsheet)

A live link per cellar. The seller's own list, every line, both numbers, the
evidence count per line, the exclusions named. Per-line **Accept** or **Decline**.
A running total that updates as they tick. Expires in seven days and re-prices
itself.

Already have: the pricing engine, the document design, the schema spec.
To build: accept/decline state per line, a shareable tokenised link, an audit of
what the seller saw and when.

This one thing replaces the entire incumbent workflow. Build it first.

### Strategy 2 · Two speeds on every cellar

| | Paid now | Paid on sale |
|---|---|---|
| Seller receives | ~65% of market | ~74% of market |
| Timing | On collection | On sale, typically 30–60 days |
| Who holds the risk | You | Seller |

Fanatics' model, applied to wine. Most sellers of inherited or surplus cellars
take the money. The ones with time take the upside — and tell you so, which is
free market intelligence.

### Strategy 3 · The Thursday List (the buy side)

One list a week, same day, to a closed buyer group. Twenty to forty lines, not
five hundred. Each line: wine, vintage, bottles, location, price, and the market
reference it sits below. First come, first served, 24 hours, then it goes wider.

Channel: **WhatsApp broadcast plus a link.** Not email. The message is the
doorbell: "Thursday list is live — 31 lines, Burgundy grand cru and mature
Bordeaux, from £64 a bottle." The link is the product.

### Strategy 4 · Buyer wants, matched automatically

Every buyer states what they want: regions, producers, price ceilings per
bottle, formats. When a cellar lands, each buyer gets only their matches —
"nine lines from a new Hong Kong cellar fit your list" — before anyone sees the
rest. This is the mechanism that makes a 561-line cellar sellable in days
rather than months, and it is cheap to build because the identity layer already
resolves what each line *is*.

### Strategy 5 · The membership, once there's a rhythm

Not yet — earn it first. After roughly ten cellars and a dozen regular buyers:
an application-reviewed buyer side, a fee that pre-qualifies, first look at new
cellars, and the valuation tool free to members. Crurated's €300 does more work
as a filter than as revenue.

### Strategy 6 · Custody, eventually

Whoever holds the stock sets the speed. A bonded account of your own (LCB in the
UK, a HK bonded partner) turns a brokerage into a marketplace: stock sits in your
vault, sellers press sell, buyers press buy, and nothing moves physically. This is
the step that makes the whole thing scale, and the one to take last, because it
needs working capital.

### The AI layer — where it actually earns

- **Ingestion.** A cellar arrives as any spreadsheet, a photo of a rack, or a
  warehouse PDF. 561 messy lines became a clean, priced list today; that should
  be a thirty-second upload, not an afternoon.
- **Identity.** Already built, and it's the hard part. "Chambertin Grand Cru"
  across seven domaines; Krug by edition; Courcel's two Épenots.
- **Market collection.** Six merchants, automated, politely, on a schedule.
- **Offer drafting.** The document writes itself from the priced lines.
- **Buyer matching.** Wants against new lines, with a reason for each match.
- **The seller's questions, answered on the page.** "Why is this line lower than
  the others?" answered with the evidence, by an assistant on the offer page —
  not by you at 11pm.
- **What not to use it for.** The price. The margin. The identity approval on a
  line worth four figures. The system's whole credibility rests on those being
  deterministic and traceable, which is exactly how it's built.

### The stack

| Job | Use |
|---|---|
| Offer rooms, buyer lists, portfolio views | the existing Next.js app — this is the product |
| Seller and buyer messaging | WhatsApp Business API (Twilio or 360dialog) |
| Live sales, later | Whatnot-style show, or your own stream once there's an audience |
| Payments | bank transfer for sellers; Stripe for buyer deposits |
| Storage | LCB account under your own name, when capital allows |
| Market data | the six adapters, plus Liv-ex when the volume justifies it |
| Documents | HTML link first, PDF second, never a spreadsheet |

## Part 8 · Ninety days

**Weeks 1–3.** Offer Room live, with accept/decline. Run the Hong Kong cellar
through it end to end. One real seller, one real document, one real acceptance.

**Weeks 4–6.** Buyer wants captured for the existing buyers. First Thursday List
on WhatsApp. Measure: how many lines clear in 24 hours.

**Weeks 7–9.** Two-speed pricing on the second and third cellars. Instant-offer
tier priced from the liquid lines only.

**Weeks 10–12.** Seller portfolio view — their cellar, valued, updated monthly,
free. That's the thing that brings the next cellar without a phone call.

Numbers to watch, and only these four: **days from cellar received to offer
sent**; **percentage of lines accepted**; **percentage of lines cleared within 30
days**; **margin per cellar after logistics**.

## Part 9 · What not to do

- **Don't build an auction.** You'd be competing with Bonhams and Christie's on
  their ground, with four months of float and no audience.
- **Don't open to the public.** The margin lives in a closed room. Wide
  distribution turns you into a price-comparison site with 8%.
- **Don't take exclusivity from sellers.** Let them keep their options. The
  evidence document wins the business, and a seller who shops it around and
  comes back is the best reference you'll get.
- **Don't quote a lump sum for a cellar.** It's the one thing the whole system is
  built to beat. Per line, with evidence, always.
- **Don't let AI touch a price.** The moment a seller finds a number nobody can
  trace, the differentiator is gone.

---

## The one-sentence version

Every incumbent asks sellers to trust them; the engine already built means you
can show them instead — so package it as a live offer link, give every seller a
choice between cash now and more later, and put one list a week in front of a
closed room of buyers on WhatsApp.
